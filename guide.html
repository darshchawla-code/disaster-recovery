AidAtlas user guide

AidAtlas helps disaster managers answer three questions before and during a disaster: where will it hurt, what will people need, and how do we get it to them fastest and fairly? This guide explains every screen, button and term in plain language.

1. Getting started

  1. Open index.html (the home page) or app.html (straight to the map) in Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox. Double-clicking the file is enough; nothing needs installing.
  2. You must be online. AidAtlas pulls live disaster data, satellite images, roads and population from public sources each time you use it.
  3. Pick a mode: Live, Plan, History or Risk (sections 3–6).
Use the files from your project folder. Each page is one self-contained file, so you can copy or email it, but keep index.html, app.html and guide.html together so the links between them work.

3. Live mode: disasters happening now

AidAtlas reads three official feeds every time you open Live mode and again every 10 minutes:

  • GDACS (UN and European Commission): earthquakes, cyclones, floods, volcanoes, droughts and wildfires, with Green, Orange or Red alert levels.
  • USGS (US Geological Survey): every earthquake of magnitude 4.5 or more in the last week, with its PAGER alert.
  • NASA EONET: events seen by satellites, such as wildfires, storms and volcanoes.

Events are ranked by the Unified Severity Index and the Top 5 are shown in the panel and as numbered green dots on the map. Smaller green dots are other current events.

What to do

  1. Read the list: each card shows the event name, type, alert colour, country, size, how long ago it was updated and the data source. The number on the right is the severity score (0 to 1).
  2. Click a card or a numbered dot to open its plan.
  3. Press Generate situation report for a one-page briefing on all five events (section 9).
For cyclones, GDACS reports the strongest wind the storm ever had. If the storm has since weakened (its current alert is lower), AidAtlas plans with the current strength and says so in the plan heading and in Data notes.

4. Plan mode: “what if a disaster hit here?”

Use this to prepare before anything happens, or to run drills.

  1. Type a place in the search box and press Enter, then choose the right result. You can also just click on the map. The point turns green.
  2. Choose the Hazard and its strength:
    • Earthquake: magnitude (4.5–9.5) and depth in km.
    • Tropical cyclone: maximum wind speed in km/h, and Hours until peak (time until landfall).
    • Flood, wildfire, volcano, drought: an intensity from 0.1 (mild) to 1 (extreme). Floods also take hours until peak; wildfires and droughts take an area in km².
  3. Set the Supply level if you know your stocks are higher or lower than normal.
  4. Press Build relief plan. In 20–60 seconds you get the full plan plus recommended storage sites.

5. History mode: what happened here before

  1. Search a place (or click the map).
  2. AidAtlas collects major disasters within 300 km: GDACS Orange and Red events since 2000, USGS earthquakes of magnitude 5.5+ since 1900, and NASA EONET events. Duplicates are merged.
  3. The 5 most recent are listed newest first, with date, size and distance. Click one to replay it: AidAtlas runs the full plan as if it were happening now, so you can see which routes and storage sites would have worked best.
  4. Generate history report summarises the record and what it means for preparedness.

6. Risk mode: where disaster is most likely to hurt

  1. Search a city or region. AidAtlas draws a 6 × 6 grid of squares (about 20 km each, 120 km across).
  2. It reads the earthquake record since 1973, 20 years of river flow, past storms, floods, fires and volcanoes, where people live, the nearest hospitals, and national data from the World Bank.
  3. Each square gets a risk score. Darker orange means higher risk. The Top 5 are outlined in black and listed with their main danger and the four parts of the score (H, E, V, LCC).
  4. Click an area to open a plan for its most likely disaster.
  5. Plan storage for all five finds the best places for relief warehouses that reach all five areas quickly (purple diamonds and dashed lines).
  6. Generate preparedness report gives a step-by-step preparation list.

7. Reading a plan

Every mode ends in the same plan view. From top to bottom:

Heading

The event name, type, alert colour, country, size and source. Below it: the impact radius, the number of demand zones, how many supply stores are open and how many hospitals receive patients. “T+0 h · epoch 0” is the plan time (see epoch).

Key actions now

The most important steps for the next 6 hours in plain words, also shown in the box on the map: warn people, open shelters, move the injured, dispatch the first supplies, arrange air drops, ask for outside help and stock storage sites. Click a line to fly to the place. Generate action report turns these and much more into a printable report.

Forecast

Four boxes: Affected, Displaced, Injured, Fatalities. The big number is the most likely value (P50); the small line is the range (P10–P90). Plan for the higher end.

Names and addresses. Affected areas are named after the real sectors, colonies, suburbs and villages that OpenStreetMap has at that spot (for example “Sector 29” or “DLF Phase 1”); an area also lists the nearby localities it covers. Supply stores, hospitals and recommended storage sites show a street address once it has been looked up (it fills in within a few seconds after the plan appears). Hover any dot to see its address, click it for coordinates and an Open in Google Maps link for directions, and zoom in (street level) to see name labels on the map. The action report repeats every address and ends with a Where everything is table.

Under the boxes, the evidence card shows what agencies have already confirmed (reported impact), the USGS PAGER loss estimate or alert for earthquakes, the flood type, and where the population and water figures come from (WorldPop, the GDACS flood outline, river flow). Reported counts are minimums, so the forecast never goes below them. If the evidence card says the model alone gave a much lower number, trust the reported number.

This epoch's allocation

  • Bars by item (water, food, shelter, medical, personnel): the share of this 6-hour period's need that can be delivered from local stock with local vehicles. Under each bar: the amount delivered and the amount needed.
  • Total cost: fuel, crew time, dispatch, loading and congestion cost of the plan, compared with a simple “send everything from the nearest store” plan (naive cost).
  • Vehicle trips: how many trips are needed, and how many were saved by sharing trucks (milk runs).
  • What would close the gap: how many more trucks or buses would let you move stock that is already available, and how much extra stock to ask for from state or national reserves.
  • The grey line names the calculation method, how long it took, routing progress and any air drops.

Demand zones by need · Supply stores

Zones are sorted by need score, with people affected and critical coverage. Click a zone ID to zoom to it. Stores shows how much of each store's stock and truck time the plan uses. You can replace assumed stock with your own (section 12).

Recommended storage

In Plan, History and Risk modes AidAtlas suggests where to keep relief stock before a disaster (purple diamonds). Each site is outside the expected heavy-damage area and covers as much need as possible within the coverage time. The table shows how much to keep at each site: 3 days of supplies for the worst likely case.

Change log, Data notes, Sources

The change log lists every re-plan with its reason, cost change, which zones gained or lost trips, and how unmet water changed. Data notes flag anything estimated because a data source was unavailable. Sources lists where the data came from.

8. Responding to change

Disasters change by the hour. Every action below re-plans immediately and is recorded in the change log.

Button or actionUse it whenWhat AidAtlas does
Advance 6 hA 6-hour period has passed.Records this period's deliveries as received, takes them out of store stock, adds routine resupply, lets damaged roads recover a little, moves the forecast forward and plans the next period. See rolling horizon.
Close a roadA road is blocked.Click the road on the map. A ✕ marks it, and every route within 250 m of it is replaced by a detour or, if none exists, an air drop.
Disaster spreadsA new area is hit.Click the new area. It becomes a new demand zone with its own forecast, and supplies are shared with it.
New supplies arriveA convoy, airlift or donation lands.Click where it arrived. It becomes a new store and the plan decides where its stock is needed most.
Aftershock / New damageConditions get worse.Roads near the centre are damaged further and nearby demand rises 15 %.
Congestion on/offRoads are jammed.All travel times rise 40 %, and convoys are spread out to avoid jams.
Clear closuresRoads have reopened.Removes all ✕ marks and re-plans.
Supply level sliderYour stock is larger or smaller than normal.Scales every store's assumed stock (0.25× to 4×).
Policy sliderYou want to change the balance between cost and fairness.Left = cheapest plan; right = shares supplies more evenly by need even if it costs more. See policy weights.
Click a green zone → Demand surgeAn area needs more than forecast.Raises that zone's demand by 50 % and sends more there.
Green zone → Mark served / Needs aid againAn area has enough, or needs help again.A served zone gets nothing new, so its share goes elsewhere. This prevents duplicate deliveries.
Green zone → Field reportA team on the ground reports how bad it is.Move the slider to the observed severity (0 none to 1 total destruction), choose who reported it, press Apply. The forecast is corrected; trained assessors count more than crowd reports. See Bayesian update.

Red store → Truck breakdownA vehicle is lost.That store has one truck fewer.

9. Action reports

Every mode has a report button. Reports are written in plain language for the Met department, district administration, disaster management authority or hospital managers. They use only the numbers on screen; nothing is made up.

WhereButtonWhat it contains
Any planGenerate action report (also Full action report in the map box)One-paragraph summary · areas that need help first · Do now: next 6 hours (numbered steps: warnings, control room, rescue, shelters, moving the injured, exactly which supplies to send from where, air drops, closed roads, what to ask for) · next 1–3 days · supplies table · what could change the plan · how the numbers were made.
Live listGenerate situation reportThe top 5 events with where, what, alert level and three first actions each.
History listGenerate history reportThe record of past events, the most common disaster type and what to prepare for.
Risk listGenerate preparedness reportThe five highest-risk areas, why they are risky, and a preparation checklist including storage sites if you planned them.

In the report window: Print / save as PDF opens a clean page and your browser's print dialog (choose “Save as PDF” to keep a file). Copy text copies plain text for email or WhatsApp. Download saves the report as a web page you can attach or open later.

3D map

The map opens in 2D, which loads fastest. Press 3D on the 2D | 3D switch in the top-right corner of the map to change to the 3D map; the 3D engine is downloaded the first time you do this (a few seconds), then switching is instant. Your choice is remembered on this device, so if you leave it on 3D the map opens in 3D next time. Both views always show the same plan, events and risk grid. 3D works in all current desktop and phone browsers.

Moving around. Drag to move. Scroll or pinch to zoom. Right-drag (or Ctrl+drag) to tilt and rotate; on a phone, drag with two fingers. The compass button (top right) resets north; the arrow button resets the tilt. Hover any column, dot or line for a summary, and click it for the same actions as in 2D (zone controls, store on/offline, address and Open in Google Maps). Links can force a view: add &view=3d or &view=2d. If a computer has no WebGL, or the 3D library cannot be downloaded, AidAtlas shows the 2D map and says why.

11. The Models panel

Press Models in the top bar, any underlined model name in the legend, or a link next to a section heading. A drawer opens with each model's formula and a table of the actual numbers used in this plan: severity score, forecast, need scores, demand, every shipment, congestion, shared trucks, every route, casualties, storage sites and risk. This is the proof behind each recommendation.

12. Settings and your own data

  • TomTom traffic key: optional, for real traffic colours.
  • Need-score weights: how much severity, population at risk, vulnerability, access difficulty and supplies already received count. They are rescaled to add up to 1.
  • Storage sites to recommend (P) and Coverage time: how many storage sites to suggest and how quickly each must reach its areas.

Using your real stock

Store locations come from OpenStreetMap, but their stock is assumed by store type. For real planning, load your inventory: in a plan, under Supply stores, choose Import your inventory and pick a CSV file with these columns:

name, lat, lon, type, water_l, food_kg, tents, medkits, staff, trucks, buses, ambulances, beds

type is one of warehouse, hospital, fire_station, police. A template is in examples/inventory-template.csv. Common spellings are recognised too (latitude, lng, water, ambulance, personnel…).

Live inventory from a Google Sheet. Keep your stock in a Google Sheet with the same columns, share it as “Anyone with the link can view” (or File → Share → Publish to web → CSV), then open Workspace → Inventory source, paste the link, press Test link and Save source. Any CSV or JSON link works too. From then on every plan you open uses your real stock and vehicles instead of assumed ones, and re-reads the sheet every 5–60 minutes while the plan is open (you choose). A plan also has a Sync stock and fleet now button. Each sync is written to the decision log.

12a. Workspace: people, roles, saved plans and alerts

The Workspace button (top right) shows who you are and your role. The small badge next to it says Viewer, Planner or Admin.

RoleCan
ViewerSee plans, maps and reports, download plan files and reports. Cannot change or save a plan.
PlannerEverything a viewer can, plus change plans (advance time, close roads, field reports…), save plans, add watch areas and connect inventory.
AdminEverything a planner can, plus manage the team and the shared back-end connection.

Without a back end, the role is a setting on this browser: useful on a shared control-room computer, but anyone at the computer can change it. For real access control use the shared back end below.

Saving plans and the decision log

  • In any plan, type a name and press Save plan. Saved plans appear in Plan mode → Saved plans; click one to reopen it. Saving again overwrites it and keeps the previous version; Save as new makes a copy.
  • A saved plan keeps everything it was built from (towns, stores, population counts, agency reports, travel times) and every decision made since. Reopening rebuilds it exactly, without downloading anything again, and replays the decisions in order. Deliveries already made are reproduced exactly; the current 6-hour period is re-optimised.
  • Download plan file saves the same thing as a .json file you can e-mail. Anyone can open it with Plan mode → Open a plan file.
  • The decision log lists every change: time, plan hour, who, role, what changed. Type a reason in Note for the log before you act and it is stored with that action. Download decision log (CSV) gives an audit trail for after-action reviews.

Watch areas and alerts

  • In Live mode, move the map to the place you are responsible for, open Add a watch area, give it a name, a radius, the alert level that matters (Orange or Red, Red only, or any) and the hazards, then press Add.
  • Each watch area lists the live events inside it at or above its level. Click one to open its plan.
  • Turn on browser alerts gives a pop-up notification while AidAtlas is open (checked every 10 minutes). Each event alerts once per level.
  • Alerts when AidAtlas is closed: press Export for scheduled alerts to download watch-areas.json, commit it to the repository, and add a repository secret ALERT_WEBHOOK_URL (an incoming-webhook link from Slack, Microsoft Teams, Google Chat or Discord; Teams and Google Chat can forward to e-mail). The included GitHub Action checks every hour and posts each new match once. You can also run node tools/watch-alerts.js watch-areas.json yourself.

Shared back end (optional, for teams)

  1. Create a free project at supabase.com. In its SQL editor, run the file supabase/schema.sql once.
  2. In AidAtlas: Workspace → Shared back end, paste the project URL and the public “anon” key, press Connect.
  3. Type your e-mail, press Send code, type the 6-digit code from the e-mail, press Sign in. (In Supabase → Authentication → Email templates, make sure the template shows {{ .Token }} so the e-mail contains the code.)
  4. Create a team: you become its admin. Invite colleagues by e-mail with a role; they join when they sign in with that e-mail.

Saved plans and watch areas then live in the team's database and are shared by everyone in the team. Roles are enforced by the database (row-level security), so a viewer cannot change plans even with technical tricks. People outside the team see nothing.

13. Shareable links

Add these to app.html to open a view directly, for example in a briefing email:

  • ?mode=live&event=top: today's most severe event.
  • ?mode=plan&q=Gurugram&hazard=EQ&mag=7.2: a planning scenario. Hazards: EQ earthquake, TC cyclone, FL flood, WF wildfire, VO volcano, DR drought.
  • ?mode=history&q=Kathmandu and ?mode=risk&q=Manila.
  • Add &view=3d or &view=2d to any link to open the 3D or flat map.

14. Glossary of every term

A–CD–FG–LM–OP–RS–Z

Access difficulty (acc)
How hard a zone is to reach: travel time from the nearest open store, made longer by road damage, then scaled 0 (easiest) to 1 (hardest). Hard-to-reach zones get a higher need score so they are not forgotten.Need score, Models → Need score
Affected
People who feel strong effects and need some help: strong shaking, damaging wind, flood water or smoke. Not everyone affected needs shelter.Forecast boxes, reports
Aftershock / New damage
A button that simulates things getting worse: more road damage near the centre and 15 % more demand nearby.Respond to change
Air drop
A zone that no truck can reach because every road route passes a closed road or a very badly damaged stretch. Supplies must go by helicopter or air.Map (red dotted line), allocation line, reports
Alert level (Green / Orange / Red)
The official GDACS alert: Green = low humanitarian impact expected, Orange = medium, Red = high, international help likely needed. USGS earthquakes have their own PAGER alert (green, yellow, orange, red).Coloured dot next to events
Ambulances / casualty transport
About a quarter of the injured need a hospital. AidAtlas sends them to hospitals outside the damage zone, as many as free beds and ambulance time allow, closest first because survival falls the longer the trip takes.Pink lines, Models → Casualties
Bayesian update (field report)
A standard way to combine the forecast with what people on the ground report. The new estimate lies between the two, closer to whichever is more reliable. A trained assessor (±0.10) moves it more than a crowd report (±0.20).Green zone popup → Field report
BPR congestion
A road-traffic formula from the US Bureau of Public Roads: travel time rises sharply as more vehicles use the same road. AidAtlas uses it to spread convoys instead of jamming one access road.Models → Congestion
Branch and bound
An exact method for optimisation problems with whole numbers (you cannot send 2.4 trucks). Used when the problem is small enough; otherwise see LP relaxation + round-up repair.
Clarke–Wright savings
A classic method for combining part-loads into one truck that visits several areas, when that saves distance and fits the truck and the time window.Models → Consolidation
Closure
A road you marked as blocked (✕). Any route passing within 250 m is rejected.
Congestion toggle
Makes all travel 40 % slower, as in heavy traffic or evacuation jams.
Consolidation / trips saved
Vehicles removed from the road by sharing trucks between nearby zones.
Cost (total)
Estimated money cost of this 6-hour plan in US dollars: $1.20/km and $40/hour per truck, $150 per dispatch, $0.02 per kg loaded, plus the extra delay cost of congestion. These are editable assumptions, used to compare plans rather than as a budget.
Coverage time
The maximum drive time from a storage site to an area for that area to count as covered. Default 90 minutes.
Critical coverage
The share of a zone's water and medical need (the life-saving items) delivered this period.
Damage curve (fragility curve)
A curve, built from past disasters, that turns shaking or wind strength into the share of buildings damaged and the share of people affected, displaced, injured or killed.
Data notes
Warnings when a data source did not answer and AidAtlas used an estimate instead, for example modelled staging sites when no facilities were found.
Demand
How much of each item a zone needs this period. It is based on Sphere standards, weighted across the three scenarios, raised by surges and lowered by what has already been received.
Depth
How far below ground an earthquake starts. Shallow earthquakes (under 20 km) do more damage.
Displaced
People who cannot stay in their homes and need shelter.
Epoch / T+ hours
AidAtlas plans in 6-hour periods called epochs. “T+12 h · epoch 2” means 12 hours after the plan started.
Equity terms
The parts of the allocation model that protect fairness: minimax, Gini and service floor.
Fatalities
Expected deaths. Earthquake deaths use a curve like the USGS PAGER model and depend on the country's vulnerability, and are kept inside the PAGER alert band when USGS has issued one. Flood deaths depend on the flood type. When agencies have reported deaths, the number shown is never lower than the reported count.
WorldPop population
A map of where people live, in 100 m squares, from the University of Southampton (2020 edition). AidAtlas counts the people within a few kilometres of each area (half the distance to the next area, between 1.5 and 15 km) instead of guessing from town names. If WorldPop does not answer, OpenStreetMap populations or a default per town type are used and the data notes say so.
Flood outline
The area GDACS marks as affected by a flood. AidAtlas looks for towns inside it rather than in a circle around a single point.
River flow (GloFAS)
How much water the nearest river carries now compared with its usual level over the past year, from the Global Flood Awareness System. Twice the usual level means water is leaving the river bed; five times is a major flood. Used for live floods only.
Decision log
The list of every change made to a plan, with time, who made it, their role and an optional note. Downloadable as CSV.
Watch area
A circle you care about (for example your district). AidAtlas tells you when a live event inside it reaches the alert level you chose.
Role (viewer, planner, admin)
What a person can do. Viewers read; planners also change and save plans; admins also manage the team.
Flood type (flash / river)
Flash floods (in steep valleys, from a glacial lake bursting, a cloudburst or a dam failure) arrive in minutes and kill about 1 in 100 people in their path. River floods rise over days and kill about 1 in 10,000, but displace many more people. AidAtlas decides the type from the event name or the height difference of the land within 20 km (800 m or more = flash).
Reported impact
Deaths, missing, injured and displaced people counted by governments and agencies, read from GDACS (Sendai-framework impact reports). These are confirmed minimums that grow over time. Missing people are added to the worst-case (P90) death toll.
Fleet / truck hours
The vehicles at each store (assumed by store type unless you import your own) and the hours they can drive in 6 hours. “Truck hours used” shows how busy they are.
Service floor
A rule that every zone should get at least half (φ = 0.5) of its water and medical need before any zone gets more, wherever possible.
GDACS
Global Disaster Alert and Coordination System, run by the UN and the European Commission. AidAtlas's main live and historical source.
Generalised cost
How AidAtlas chooses between road routes: fuel cost per km plus crew cost for the drive time, with the time made longer on damaged roads.
Gini (shortage inequality)
A measure of how unequal shortages are between zones: 0 = everyone short by the same share, higher = some zones much worse off. AidAtlas keeps it low.
GloFAS / river discharge
The Global Flood Awareness System's daily estimate of river flow (cubic metres per second), used in Risk mode to judge flood danger.
Gutenberg–Richter / b-value
A law of earthquakes: small ones are much more common than big ones. Fitting it to the local record gives how often a magnitude-6 or bigger quake happens (λ(M≥6), per year). The b-value is the slope, usually near 1.
Gumbel / Q100
A statistical method for extremes. From 20 years of yearly peak river flows it estimates the 100-year flood (Q100), the flow with a 1 % chance of being exceeded in any year.
H, E, V, LCC
The four parts of the risk score, each from 0 to 1: Hazard (how likely disasters are), Exposure (how many people live there), Vulnerability (rural share and national poverty, so how badly people are hurt), Lack of Coping Capacity (distance to hospital and hospital beds per person).
Hospitals (H)
Hospitals from OpenStreetMap outside the heavy-damage zone. Free beds are assumed to be 30 % of capacity (150 beds if unknown).
Impact radius
The distance from the centre within which at least 10 % of people are expected to be affected.
INFORM
The risk index used by the UN and the European Commission. Risk = (hazard and exposure) × vulnerability × lack of coping capacity, combined so a low value in one part cannot hide a high value in another.
Injured
People expected to need medical care. For earthquakes, about 3.5 injured for each death (WHO ratio).
kL (kilolitre)
1,000 litres of water, about one tonne. An 8-tonne truck carries 8 kL.
Lethality prior
How deadly each disaster type usually is, from historical loss records: earthquake 1.0, cyclone 0.9, flood 0.8, volcano 0.7, drought 0.6, wildfire 0.5. Part of the severity score.
LP relaxation + round-up repair
The fast method used for big problems: solve as if half trucks were allowed, round each up to whole trucks, remove extra trucks where a store does not have enough driving time (biggest first, so small zones keep theirs), then recalculate the loads.
Magnitude (Mw)
The size of an earthquake. Each step up releases about 32 times more energy. 5 = moderate, 6 = strong, 7 = major, 8+ = great.
Mark served
Tells AidAtlas a zone has enough. Its share goes to others, avoiding duplicate deliveries.
MCLP (Maximal Covering Location Problem)
The method that chooses storage sites: from many candidate places, pick the few that bring the most people in need within the coverage time. Ties go to the sites with the shortest total drive time (the p-median rule).
Medical kits / modules
A planning unit of about 50 kg: one per 50 injured plus one per 1,000 affected people for basic care.
Milk run
One truck delivering to several zones on one trip, shown as a dashed line.
MILP (allocation model)
Mixed-Integer Linear Programme: the optimisation model that decides how much of each item goes from each store to each zone and how many vehicles to send, at the lowest cost, while protecting the neediest zones.
Minimax (worst-off zone)
The model makes the worst-off zone as well off as possible, with higher-need zones held to smaller shortages. This stops a big or cheap-to-reach town taking everything.
MMI (shaking intensity)
Modified Mercalli Intensity: how strongly the ground shakes at a place, from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction). VI = strong, some damage; VIII = severe; IX+ = violent.
Monte Carlo
Running the forecast 400 times with slightly different inputs (size, population, building strength) to see the range of possible outcomes.
Naive cost (C₀)
The cost of simply sending everything from each zone's nearest store. Used as a yardstick; the optimised plan is usually cheaper and fairer.
Need score
A number from 0 to 1 saying how urgently a zone needs help: 35 % severity, 20 % people at risk, 20 % vulnerability, 15 % access difficulty, minus 10 % for supplies already received. Population counts less than severity on purpose, so a big town with light damage does not outrank a small town with heavy damage.
OpenStreetMap (OSM)
The free world map that supplies towns, populations, hospitals, fire and police stations and warehouses.
OSRM (routing)
Open Source Routing Machine: finds the fastest road routes and drive times on real roads, using a fast exact shortest-path method (Contraction Hierarchies).
P10 / P50 / P90
The forecast range. P50 is the middle (most likely) value. There is a 10 % chance the real number is below P10 and a 10 % chance it is above P90. Plan stock for P90.
PAGER
USGS's Prompt Assessment of Global Earthquakes for Response, which issues green, yellow, orange or red alerts for expected deaths and losses. When USGS has published its full loss estimate, AidAtlas uses PAGER's death range instead of its own curve (PAGER is calibrated country by country on past earthquakes) and uses PAGER's count of people exposed to each shaking level to work out how many are affected.
Personnel
Relief workers: one per 20 injured plus one per 500 displaced. They travel by responder bus.
Policy weights / Policy slider
How much the plan values fairness compared with cost. Moving the slider right raises the weights on the worst-off zone (δ), inequality (β) and the service floor (μ).
Provenance labels (osm, modelled, assumed, live, historical)
Where a number came from: osm = OpenStreetMap, modelled = estimated by AidAtlas, assumed = a planning assumption you should replace with real data, live / historical = official feeds.
Received
Supplies a zone has already been given. They are subtracted from its demand so nothing is sent twice.
Risk score (R)
A number from 0 to 1 for each grid square in Risk mode, combining H, E, V and LCC with the INFORM method.
Road damage (θ) and recovery
A number from 0 to 1 for how damaged roads are at each point, from the same shaking, wind or flood forecast. Damaged roads are slower; roads at 0.95 or above are treated as blocked. Each 6 hours about 8 % of the damage is cleared.
Rolling horizon
Plan the next 6 hours, carry them out, see what actually happened, then plan again. Only the next period's decisions are acted on, so the plan follows reality instead of a guess made on day one.
Scenarios (low / base / high)
The P10, P50 and P90 forecasts, weighted 30 % / 40 % / 30 % when working out expected demand.
Severity
The share of buildings expected to be heavily damaged in a zone, from 0 to 1. Field reports can correct it.
Shortage ratio
The share of a zone's need that is not delivered (0 = fully supplied, 1 = nothing delivered).
Sphere standards
International minimum standards for humanitarian response: at least 15 litres of water per person per day, 2,100 kcal of food (about 0.6 kg), and 3.5 m² of covered space per person (one family tent per 5 people).
Staging site (modelled)
A temporary supply point AidAtlas adds outside the damage zone when not enough real facilities are found. In reality this would be a stadium, depot or open ground where supplies are gathered.
Supply store
Any place relief can come from: warehouse, hospital, fire station, police station, staging site or a new supply arrival.
Supply level
A multiplier on all assumed stock (0.25× to 4×).
Demand surge
Raises one zone's demand by 50 % when it turns out worse than forecast.
Survival weight
How the casualty plan values a trip: e−time/1 hour, so a 30-minute trip scores 0.61 and a 2-hour trip 0.14. This sends patients to the nearest hospital with free beds first.
Tents
Family tents for 5 people each, needed for displaced people.
USI (Unified Severity Index)
AidAtlas's single score for ranking different kinds of disasters: 50 % agency alert level (giving the current alert more weight than the original one), 35 % size of the event, 15 % how deadly that type usually is. From 0 to 1.
Vehicles in transit
Optional moving dots showing where each dispatched vehicle would be along its planned route. Simulated, not GPS. Off by default; turn on in the legend.
Vulnerability (vul / V)
How badly people are likely to be hurt by the same event: higher in rural areas and in countries with lower income and fewer hospital beds (World Bank data).
Wind speed / Holland wind model
For cyclones: maximum sustained wind in km/h. The wind is strongest within about 30 km of the centre and falls off with distance. Above 120 km/h damage is widespread.
Demand zone
A town, village or district sector that needs aid, built from OpenStreetMap places within the impact radius. Big cities are split into four parts. Up to 10 zones, chosen by how many people are affected.

15. Troubleshooting

ProblemFix
Page has no colours or layout; buttons do nothingYou opened a copy without its code (for example from a chat download folder). Open index.html or app.html from your project folder.
“Live feeds could not be reached”Check your internet connection and reload. Office networks sometimes block gdacs.org or usgs.gov.
Status pill stays on “Finding towns, hospitals…” for a long timeThe free OpenStreetMap servers are busy. AidAtlas waits up to 20 seconds, then tries a second source, then uses staging sites. This is noted under Data notes.
Routes are straight lines marked “estimated”The routing server did not answer. Drive times are estimated at 40 km/h. Try again later.
Allocation bars are very lowThat is the result, not an error: local stock or vehicles are much smaller than the need. Read “What would close the gap” and ask for outside help, raise the Supply level, or import your real inventory.
The map stays flat or says “3D map unavailable”The browser has WebGL turned off (some remote desktops and locked-down office PCs), or jsdelivr.net is blocked. The 2D map works fully. In Chrome, check chrome://gpu and turn on “Use graphics acceleration when available”.
3D map feels slowPress 2D on the switch at the top right of the map, or zoom out a little: terrain and buildings are the heaviest parts.
Print button does nothingYour browser blocked the pop-up. Allow pop-ups for this page, or use Download and print the saved file.

16. Data sources and limits

  • Hazards: GDACS, USGS, NASA EONET. Places, hospitals, stores: OpenStreetMap (Overpass, Nominatim). Roads and drive times: OSRM. Weather, river flow, elevation: Open-Meteo and GloFAS. Country data: World Bank. Satellite images: Esri World Imagery. 3D terrain: Mapzen Terrain Tiles on AWS Open Data. 3D buildings: OpenFreeMap (OpenStreetMap). 3D engine: MapLibre GL. Traffic (optional): TomTom.
  • Forecasts are estimates. The earthquake model was checked against Nepal 2015 and Türkiye 2023 (the real death tolls fall inside its forecast range), but local building quality can change results a lot.
  • Stock and vehicles at real facilities are assumptions until you import your own inventory.
  • The free public map and routing servers have rate limits. For an operations centre, run your own copies.
  • Always confirm with the national meteorological department and disaster management authority before acting.
